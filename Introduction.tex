\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{\protect\numberline{}Introduction}

Over-the-Air (OTA) Computing represents a paradigm shift in how distributed
computations are executed across wireless networks. By exploiting the
superposition property of the wireless Multiple-Access Channel (MAC),
\gls{ota} allows
simultaneous transmissions from multiple sensing nodes to be
aggregated in the air,
directly yielding a desired nomographic function of the distributed data. This
approach inherently couples communication and computation, breaking
the separation
principle to achieve unprecedented spectral efficiency and reduced
latency. These
properties are increasingly critical for rapidly scaling applications such as
Federated Learning (FL), distributed consensus, and real-time sensor analytics.

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
Traditional orthogonal access schemes, such as Time-Division Multiple
Access (\gls{tdma}),
suffer from severe scalability bottlenecks because their resource
requirements scale
linearly with the number of transmitting nodes
\cite{sahin_survey_2023}. In computation-oriented
networks where the receiver is interested only in a function of the
data (e.g., the arithmetic
mean) rather than the individual messages themselves, orthogonal
separation proves highly
suboptimal \cite{nazer_reliable_nodate, goldenbaum_robust_2013}.
\gls{ota} computation leverages
analog signal superposition to compute these functions directly at
the physical layer,
achieving a computation rate that is theoretically independent of the
network size \cite{goldenbaum_nomographic_2015}.
However, achieving this theoretical efficiency in practice requires
precise control over the
transmitted waveforms and rigorous synchronization across spatially
distributed transmitters.

\subsection{Problem Statement and Research Objectives}
While the theoretical foundations of computation over MAC are well-established
\cite{nazer_reliable_nodate, goldenbaum_analyzing_2011}, translating
these scaling
laws into practical realizations remains a formidable challenge. The
primary focus of this
thesis is to bridge the gap between idealized \gls{ota} theory and
physical deployment. By
systematically implementing \gls{ota} architectures across multiple
abstraction layers---ranging
from mathematical formulations and discrete-time MATLAB simulations
to dynamical Simulink
models and ultimately Software-Defined Radio (SDR) platforms---this
work aims to quantify
how different modeling fidelities capture the impact of physical
channel impairments.
Specifically, the objectives are to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Establish a rigorous ideal \gls{ota} baseline and validate
        its underlying physics.
    \item Systematically model and quantify the degradation caused by
        severe channel impairments, particularly multipath fractional
        delay and asynchronous Carrier Frequency Offsets (CFO).
    \item Evaluate established filtering and equalization techniques
        (such as squaring loops, Costas loops, and non-coherent
        envelope detection) to combat these uncoordinated impairments.
    \item Lay the groundwork for a robust \gls{sdr} implementation
        using GNU Radio that addresses hardware-level synchronization limits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Thesis Contributions and Organization}
This thesis contributes a comprehensive, cross-platform methodology
for evaluating \gls{ota}
computation under non-ideal physical conditions. The structure of the
thesis is organized
as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ideal Over-the-Air Computation Baseline:}
        Establishes the fundamental physical and mathematical models,
        exploring Double-Sideband Amplitude Modulation, matched
        filtering, and the theoretical Mean Squared Error (MSE)
        scaling under pure Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN).
    \item \textbf{Multipath Propagation, Fractional Delay, and
        Intersymbol Interference:} Dissects the vulnerability of
        \gls{ota} computation to multipath propagation and
        asynchronous CFO, providing detailed mathematical
        derivations, discrete-time simulations, and continuous-time
        Simulink block-level architectures that expose the mechanics
        of intersymbol interference and phase misalignment.
    \item \textbf{Experimental Implementation} (forthcoming):
        Transitions the modeled architectures into hardware,
        detailing the GNU Radio flowgraphs and the practical hurdles
        of \gls{sdr} deployment.
    \item \textbf{Experimental Results and Discussion:} Provides a
        comparative analysis of the simulation models and
        equalization strategies, alongside an evaluation framework
        for the experimental \gls{sdr} findings.
    \item \textbf{Conclusion and Future Work:} Synthesizes the
        findings and proposes advanced adaptive equalization schemes,
        such as Unscented Kalman Filtering, for future exploration.
\end{itemize}
