\section{Ideal Over-the-Air Computation Baseline}
\label{sec:ideal_ota_baseline}
Before introducing the practical impairments inherent to physical
wireless channels—such as
multipath delay spread, intersymbol interference, carrier frequency
offsets, and time
synchronization errors—it is essential to establish an analytical and
simulation baseline of
\gls{ota} computation under ideal conditions. The foundational
premise of \gls{ota}
computing is its ability to overcome the communication bottleneck of
distributed wireless sensor
aggregation. In conventional orthogonal multi-access protocols (e.g.,
\gls{tdma} or \gls{ofdm}),
the time required to collect readings from $K$ distributed \gls{ed}s
scales linearly with the
network size, incurring a latency complexity of $\mathcal{O}(K)$. By
contrast, \gls{ota} computing
exploits the superposition property of the wireless \gls{mac} to
execute nomographic function
aggregation directly across the physical propagation medium in a
single time slot:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(K) \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}(1)
    \label{eq:ota_latency_complexity}
\end{equation}

In this idealized baseline scenario, all transmitting nodes are
assumed to be perfectly synchronized
in carrier frequency, phase, and symbol timing. The propagation
channel is modeled as a non-dispersive,
flat-fading medium corrupted solely by \gls{awgn}. This chapter
presents an end-to-end multi-tiered
experimental workflow that demonstrates this unimpaired baseline:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item The physical principles and electrical circuit equivalent
        of electromagnetic superposition;
    \item The continuous-time and passband \gls{am} mathematical formulation;
    \item Algorithmic discrete-time MATLAB modeling and multi-rate
        pulse-shaping code;
    \item Mean squared error and constellation diagnostic evaluations;
    \item Analog eye diagram analysis comparing MATLAB and Simulink dynamics;
    \item Multi-rate block-level Simulink dynamical modeling; and
    \item Software-Defined Radio (\gls{sdr}) realization and
        polyphase filter calibration in GNU Radio.
\end{enumerate}
Establishing this rigorous benchmark provides the mathematical
reference and experimental framework
repeated throughout subsequent chapters to evaluate model impurities
and channel imperfections.

\subsection{Physical Principles and Electrical Circuit Equivalent of
Electromagnetic Superposition}
\label{subsec:physical_circuit_superposition}
In conventional digital signal processors, arithmetic computation is
executed through Boolean logic
gates constructed from semiconductor transistors. In \gls{ota}
computation, however, arithmetic addition
is performed natively by free space through Maxwell's electrodynamic equations.

When $K$ distributed sensor nodes concurrently radiate
radio-frequency currents through their respective
antennas, the emitted electric field vectors
$\mathbf{E}_k(\mathbf{r}, t)$ superimpose linearly in the
spatial continuum according to the principle of electromagnetic superposition:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{E}_{\text{total}}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}
    \mathbf{E}_k(\mathbf{r}, t)
    \label{eq:maxwell_superposition}
\end{equation}
At the receive antenna aperture of the central \gls{fc}, the incident
electric field induces an open-circuit
terminal voltage proportional to the spatial integral of the field
weighted by the antenna's effective vector length.

This electrodynamic mechanism is formalized electrically in
\cref{fig:idealOTA_circuit}. Each sensor node
is modeled as a Thévenin equivalent source generating an
information-bearing voltage $v_k(t)$ with internal
impedance $R_{Sk}$. The radiating element is characterized by an
equivalent resonant $RLC$ circuit with antenna
radiation resistance $R_{\text{rad}, k}$. The wireless medium behaves
as an unguided transmission system imparting
attenuation and propagation phase shift. At the \gls{fc}, the
incoming electromagnetic wavefronts excite currents
in the receiver antenna ($R_{\text{rad, Rx}}, C_{\text{Rx}},
L_{\text{Rx}}$), developing a superposition terminal
voltage across the input impedance $Z_{\text{load}}$ of the
analog-to-digital front-end. Under ideal transmitter
pre-equalization ($h_k \to 1$), the terminal voltage equals the
direct arithmetic sum of the transmitted waveforms
plus thermal receiver noise, establishing the electrical foundation
for analog AirComp.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation of the Ideal Multiple-Access Channel}
\label{subsec:mathematical_formulation_ideal}
Consider a distributed sensory network comprising $K$ edge devices
transmitting local continuous-valued measurements
to a central \gls{fc}. Let $x_k[m]$ denote the discrete information
symbol emitted by the $k$-th device at symbol index $m$.
In uncoded analog \gls{pam}, each sensor maps its measurement
directly to the amplitude of a transmitted continuous-time pulse.
Information-theoretic analysis confirms that uncoded \gls{pam}
achieves the optimal distortion exponent for computing
nomographic sums over Gaussian \gls{mac} channels
\cite{nazer_reliable_nodate, goldenbaum_robust_2013}.

To ensure finite spectral occupancy and satisfy regulatory bandwidth
constraints, each transmitting device shapes its
symbol sequence with a \gls{rrc} pulse $p(t)$ having roll-off factor
$\alpha \in [0, 1]$ and symbol period $T_s$. The pulse
satisfies unit-energy normalization:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p^2(t) \, dt = 1
    \label{eq:rrc_continuous_unit_energy}
\end{equation}
The continuous-time baseband waveform radiated by sensor $k$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    s_k(t) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, p(t - m T_s)
    \label{eq:single_node_baseband_signal}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Passband Double-Sideband Amplitude Modulation}
\label{subsubsec:passband_am_formulation}
In practical radio frequency systems, baseband waveforms are
upconverted to a carrier frequency $f_c \gg 1/T_s$ via
Double-Sideband Amplitude Modulation (\gls{am}). Because the carrier
mixer is linear with respect to amplitude, each
sensor modulates its baseband pulse train onto an in-phase carrier:
\begin{equation}
    s_k^{\text{RF}}(t) = s_k(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) = \left[
    \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, p(t - m T_s) \right] \cos(2\pi f_c t)
    \label{eq:passband_transmitted_signal}
\end{equation}
Assuming full synchronization and ideal channel pre-equalization
($h_k(t) = \delta(t)$), the transmitted RF waveforms
superimpose instantaneously over the wireless medium:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{RF}}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K s_k^{\text{RF}}(t) +
    w_{\text{RF}}(t) = \left( \sum_{k=1}^K s_k(t) \right) \cos(2\pi
    f_c t) + w_{\text{RF}}(t)
    \label{eq:passband_superposition}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\text{RF}}(t)$ denotes zero-mean bandpass \gls{awgn} with
two-sided power spectral density $N_0 / 2$.

\subsubsection{Coherent Demodulation and Matched Filtering}
\label{subsubsec:coherent_demodulation_matched_filter}
At the \gls{fc}, coherent synchronous demodulation is performed by
mixing the incoming RF signal with a local oscillator
tuned to $2\cos(2\pi f_c t)$:
\begin{equation}
    2 r_{\text{RF}}(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) = \left( \sum_{k=1}^K s_k(t)
    \right) \left[ 1 + \cos(4\pi f_c t) \right] + 2 w_{\text{RF}}(t)
    \cos(2\pi f_c t)
    \label{eq:coherent_mixing}
\end{equation}
The double-frequency component at $2f_c$ is rejected by the
subsequent filtering stage. The baseband composite waveform
then passes through a matched filter identical to the transmitter
pulse shape, $p^*(-t) = p(t)$. The cascade of the transmit
and receive \gls{rrc} filters forms the full Nyquist Raised Cosine
(\gls{rc}) filter $r_c(t) = (p * p)(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    r_c(t) = \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{t}{T_s}\right)
    \frac{\cos\left(\frac{\pi \alpha t}{T_s}\right)}{1 -
    \left(\frac{2\alpha t}{T_s}\right)^2}
    \label{eq:nyquist_rc_pulse}
\end{equation}
The continuous-time output of the matched filter before sampling is:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, r_c(t - m
    T_s) + \tilde{w}(t)
    \label{eq:matched_filter_output_continuous}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{w}(t) = (w_{\text{bb}} * p)(t)$ is the filtered
Gaussian noise process. Sampling $y(t)$ at the symbol strobe
instants $t = n T_s$ yields:
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n] \, r_c(0) + \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{m \neq
    n} x_k[m] \, r_c((n-m)T_s) + \tilde{w}[n]
    \label{eq:sampled_matched_output}
\end{equation}
Because the Nyquist pulse satisfies the zero-\gls{isi} property
($r_c(0) = 1$ and $r_c(l T_s) = 0$ for all non-zero integers $l \neq 0$),
the intersymbol cross-terms vanish identically:
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n] + \tilde{w}[n]
    \label{eq:mac_sum}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{w}[n] \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_w^2)$. To compute the
desired arithmetic average of the $K$ sensor readings,
the \gls{fc} applies an unweighted post-processing scaling factor of $1/K$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mu}[n] = \frac{1}{K} y[n] = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n]
    + \frac{\tilde{w}[n]}{K}
    \label{eq:fc_estimated_average}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Mean Squared Error Scaling}
\label{subsubsec:theoretical_mse_scaling}
The instantaneous estimation error at symbol index $n$ is defined as
the deviation between the reconstructed sample mean
and the true arithmetic average:
\begin{equation}
    e[n] = \hat{\mu}[n] - \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n] =
    \frac{\tilde{w}[n]}{K}
    \label{eq:estimation_error_definition}
\end{equation}
The statistical \gls{mse} of the ideal Over-the-Air computation
system is obtained by taking the mathematical expectation:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MSE} = \mathbb{E}\left[ |e[n]|^2 \right] =
    \mathbb{E}\left[ \left|\frac{\tilde{w}[n]}{K}\right|^2 \right] =
    \frac{\sigma_w^2}{K^2}
    \label{eq:ideal_mse_scaling}
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:ideal_mse_scaling} reveals the fundamental cooperative
advantage of \gls{ota} aggregation: while individual sensor
signals add coherently, thermal noise adds non-coherently, resulting
in an inverse-quadratic noise suppression factor of $1/K^2$
relative to the unweighted channel noise variance
\cite{sahin_survey_2023, cao_optimized_2022}.

\subsubsection{Distributive and Linear Time-Invariant Equivalence}
\label{subsubsec:distributive_lti_proof}
A vital property of the ideal baseline concerns the mathematical
equivalence between synthesizing each sensor's modulated waveform
individually versus modulating the aggregated sequence directly. Let
$u_k(t) = \sum_m x_k[m] \delta(t - m T_s)$ represent the
ideal impulse train of sensor $k$. In the physical network, each
sensor performs pulse shaping and passband mixing locally prior
to transmission:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{agg}}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \Big( \big( u_k(t) * p(t) \big)
    \cos(2\pi f_c t) \Big)
    \label{eq:per_sensor_pipeline}
\end{equation}
Because all sensors share an identical carrier frequency and phase,
the carrier waveform factors out of the sum:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{agg}}(t) = \left( \sum_{k=1}^K \big( u_k(t) * p(t) \big)
    \right) \cos(2\pi f_c t)
    \label{eq:factored_carrier}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, convolution is a linear operator that distributes over addition:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^K \big( u_k(t) * p(t) \big) = \left( \sum_{k=1}^K
    u_k(t) \right) * p(t)
    \label{eq:lti_distributive}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:lti_distributive} into
\cref{eq:factored_carrier} yields the optimized equivalent representation:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{agg}}(t) = \left( \left[ \sum_{k=1}^K u_k(t) \right] *
    p(t) \right) \cos(2\pi f_c t)
    \label{eq:optimized_equivalent_pipeline}
\end{equation}
This proof demonstrates that under ideal synchronization and
identical channel gains, the order of operations commutes
completely. This property enables substantial computational
acceleration in discrete-time simulations: the algorithmic complexity
scales from $\mathcal{O}(K \cdot N \cdot \text{sps} \cdot
L_{\text{filt}})$ down to $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot \text{sps} \cdot
L_{\text{filt}})$,
achieving a $K$-fold reduction in runtime and memory allocation.

\subsection{Algorithmic MATLAB Implementation and Code Listings}
\label{subsec:matlab_implementation}
To evaluate these theoretical principles numerically, two distinct
discrete-time simulation architectures are modeled in MATLAB:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An unmodulated, single-sample-per-symbol baseline that
        isolates the pure mathematical summation under \gls{awgn}
        (\cref{lst:ml_awgn_fc}); and
    \item A physically complete multi-rate pipeline incorporating
        upsampling, matched \gls{rrc} pulse shaping, Double-Sideband
        \gls{am}, and coherent demodulation (\cref{lst:ml_rrc_fc}).
\end{enumerate}

    \begin{lstlisting}[style=Matlab-editor, caption={MATLAB script for ideal unmodulated OTA computation baseline.}, label={lst:ml_awgn_fc}]
        % Ideal OTA Computation Simulation - Unmodulated Baseline
        K = 20;                 % Number of sensor nodes
        N = 4000;               % Number of symbols
        SNR_dB = 20;            % Signal-to-Noise Ratio (dB)

        % Generate agricultural temperature readings around 27 deg C
        X = 27 + randn(K, N);

        % Over-The-Air superposition (Physical MAC Adder)
        Ideal_Sum = sum(X, 1);

        % Add AWGN according to measured average signal power
        Received_Signal = awgn(Ideal_Sum, SNR_dB, 'measured');

        % Fusion Center arithmetic average calculation
        Estimated_Average = Received_Signal / K;

        % Empirical Mean Squared Error computation
        MSE = immse(Estimated_Average, mean(X, 1));
        fprintf('Ideal Baseline MSE at %d dB SNR: %e\n', SNR_dB, MSE);
    \end{lstlisting}

While \cref{lst:ml_awgn_fc} demonstrates the fundamental principle in
its abstract discrete-time form, practical wireless
transceivers mandate bandlimiting and RF upconversion.
\Cref{lst:ml_rrc_fc} presents the complete multi-rate architecture.
The discrete symbol sequence is upsampled by an integer factor
\texttt{sps\_val}, convolved with a unit-energy normalized
\gls{rrc} filter, modulated onto carrier frequency $F_c = F_s / 4$,
corrupted by channel noise, coherently demodulated with
a matched \gls{rrc} filter, and downsampled at the calibrated strobe instant.

    \begin{lstlisting}[style=Matlab-editor, caption={MATLAB multi-rate script for OTA computation with matched RRC filtering and coherent AM.}, label={lst:ml_rrc_fc}]
        % Multi-Rate OTA Computation with Matched RRC and AM
        txUpSampled = upsample(X', sps_val)'; % Dimension: K x (N * sps)

        % Design Root Raised Cosine filter with unit energy normalization
        rcFilter = rcosdesign(aleph, span, sps_val, 'sqrt');

        % Commutative superposition: pulse-shape aggregate impulse sequence
        agg_impulses = sum(txUpSampled, 1);
        tx_baseband  = conv(agg_impulses, rcFilter, 'same');

        % Double-Sideband Amplitude Modulation (Fc = Fs / 4)
        t = (0:length(tx_baseband)-1) / Fs;
        carrier = cos(2 * pi * Fc * t);
        tx_passband = tx_baseband .* carrier;

        % AWGN channel addition
        rx_passband = awgn(tx_passband, current_snr, 'measured');

        % Coherent demodulation and receiver matched filtering
        rx_mixed = rx_passband .* (2 * carrier);
        rx_matched = conv(rx_mixed, rcFilter, 'same');

        % Downsample at optimal symbol strobe instant
        rx_strobed = rx_matched(1:sps_val:end);

        % Truncate transient filter edges (span / 2 symbols on each boundary)
        guard = ceil(span / 2);
        valid_idx = (guard + 1):(N - guard);
        Estimated_Average = rx_strobed(valid_idx) / K;
        True_Average      = mean(X(:, valid_idx), 1);

        % Compute Mean Squared Error
        mse_results(snr_idx, a_idx) = immse(Estimated_Average, True_Average);
    \end{lstlisting}

Parametric analysis of the 3D surface animation in
\cref{fig:mse_span_comparison} provides deep insight into the interaction
between filter span and spectral roll-off:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Span 4 (Left):} Truncating the \gls{rrc} impulse
        response to 4 symbol periods cuts off the decaying sidelobes
        abruptly. This finite-window truncation introduces subtle
        residual intersymbol ripple, causing the \gls{mse} surface to
        tilt slightly at low roll-off factors ($\alpha \to 0$).
    \item \textbf{Span 12 (Right):} Extending the filter memory to 12
        symbol durations allows the \gls{rrc} tails to decay
        naturally below $-40\text{ dB}$. Consequently, the resulting
        \gls{mse} surface is exceptionally flat across all values
        of $\alpha$, demonstrating that truncation-induced \gls{isi}
        is completely eliminated.
    \item \textbf{Oversampling Convergence:} Scaling the oversampling
        ratio from $\text{sps} = 12$ to $\text{sps} = 96$ yields
        negligible change in the \gls{mse} surface. This confirms
        that once the Nyquist sampling criterion is satisfied ($F_s > 2 B$),
        additional oversampling provides diminishing returns under
        ideal synchronization.
\end{itemize}

This apparent paradox—wherein an unfiltered channel theoretically
free of pulse-shaping distortion exhibits \emph{greater}
variance than a filtered channel—is governed by two distinct physical
and simulation mechanisms:

\subsubsection{Physical Mechanism: Matched Filtering and Noise
Bandwidth Rejection}
In communication theory, an unfiltered receiver possesses an
unconstrained noise equivalent bandwidth equal to the entire
sampling rate ($B_n = F_s / 2$). Consequently, it admits thermal
noise energy across the entire frequency spectrum.
By contrast, the Root Raised Cosine matched filter at the receiver
acts as a strictly bandlimited filter that passes only
the signal bandwidth $B = \frac{1+\alpha}{2 T_s}$, completely
rejecting out-of-band noise. By the North-Turin matched filter
theorem, convolving the received noisy waveform with $p(-t)$ executes
coherent integration: signal components add constructively,
while zero-mean noise samples partially cancel, providing an
effective processing gain proportional to the oversampling factor.

\subsubsection{Simulation Mechanism: Interaction of AWGN with Sensor
DC Pedestal}
The numerical discrepancy in MATLAB's \texttt{awgn(..., 'measured')}
function originates from the non-zero agricultural temperature
mean ($27^\circ\text{C}$). With $K = 20$ sensors, the instantaneous
aggregate baseline is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Ideal\_Sum}[n] \approx K \times 27 = 540
    \label{eq:dc_pedestal}
\end{equation}
In the unmodulated script (\cref{lst:ml_awgn_fc}), MATLAB measures
the total average signal power:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{signal}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N
    \big(\text{Ideal\_Sum}[n]\big)^2 \approx 540^2 = 291{,}600
    \label{eq:signal_power}
\end{equation}
At $\text{SNR} = 20\text{ dB}$ (a power ratio of $100:1$), MATLAB
injects noise with variance:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{noise}}^2 = \frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{100} =
    \frac{291{,}600}{100} = 2{,}916 \implies \sigma_{\text{noise}} =
    \sqrt{2{,}916} \approx 54
    \label{eq:awgn_variance}
\end{equation}
The \gls{fc} divides the noisy sum by $K = 20$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mu} = \frac{540 + w}{20} = 27 + \frac{w}{20}
    \label{eq:estimated_average_dc}
\end{equation}
The standard deviation of this estimate is:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{est}} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{noise}}}{K} = \frac{54}{20} = 2.7
    \label{eq:estimated_average_deviation}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian distribution with $\sigma_{\text{est}} = 2.7$, the
$3\sigma$ (99.7\%) confidence boundary is:
\begin{equation}
    27 \pm 3(2.7) = 27 \pm 8.1 = [18.9, 35.1]
    \label{eq:confidence_interval_unfiltered}
\end{equation}
which precisely accounts for the wide scatter observed in
\cref{fig:ml_awgn_fc_const}.

Conversely, in the multi-rate \gls{rrc} pipeline, the sequence is
upsampled by \texttt{sps\_val} $= 10$. The impulse train contains
non-zero values in only 1 out of every 10 samples. Because the
\gls{rrc} filter is normalized to unit energy ($\sum_n g[n]^2 = 1$),
the pulse energy is distributed over 10 samples, reducing the
measured average continuous power by a factor of $\text{sps}$:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{measured}} \approx \frac{(K \cdot 27)^2}{\text{sps}} =
    \frac{291{,}600}{10} = 29{,}160
    \label{eq:filtered_measured_power}
\end{equation}
Consequently, MATLAB injects a tenfold smaller noise variance:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_w^2 = \frac{29{,}160}{100} = 291.6
    \label{eq:filtered_noise_variance}
\end{equation}
Passing through the receiver matched filter preserves this unit-energy variance:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{filtered}}^2 = \sigma_w^2 \sum_{n} g^2[n] = 291.6
    \times 1 = 291.6
    \label{eq:matched_filter_noise_out}
\end{equation}
Dividing by $K = 20$ yields an estimate standard deviation of:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{est, RRC}} = \frac{\sqrt{291.6}}{20} \approx 0.85
    \label{eq:filtered_average_deviation}
\end{equation}
The resulting $3\sigma$ interval is $27 \pm 3(0.85) = [24.45,
29.55]$, yielding an exceptionally tight distribution. Furthermore,
when zero-mean residual modulation is employed ($\Delta x_k = x_k -
27$), the DC pedestal is removed entirely, centering the
constellation at $I = 0$ with $\sigma_{\text{est}} < 0.25$, as shown
in \cref{fig:ml_rrc_fc_const}.

\subsection{Eye Diagram Diagnostics: MATLAB and Simulink Comparative Analysis}
\label{subsec:eye_diagram_diagnostics}
In conventional digital communications (e.g., \gls{bpsk} or
\gls{qam}), eye diagrams exhibit an open, diamond-shaped central aperture
delimiting discrete decision thresholds ($\pm 1$). In uncoded analog
Over-the-Air computation, however, the eye diagram exhibits a
fundamentally different topology that must be interpreted as an
analog signal integrity and timing sensitivity diagnostic:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Amplitude Band:} Because sensor readings
        are continuous random variables ($X_k \sim \mathcal{N}(27, 1)$),
        the aggregated amplitude occupies a continuum of real values
        without forbidden intermediate regions, forming a smooth flowing ribbon.
    \item \textbf{Nyquist Strobe Verification:} In an ideal Nyquist
        system, the first time derivative of every pulse passes through
        zero at the nominal sampling instants:
        \begin{equation}
            \left. \frac{d p(t)}{dt} \right\vert_{t = 0} = 0
            \label{eq:nyquist_derivative}
        \end{equation}
        At integer symbol strobe instants ($t / T_s \in \{1, 2,
        \dots\}$), the trajectory curves reach local extrema or
        inflection points,
        yielding perfectly horizontal slopes that confirm zero
        intersymbol leakage.
    \item \textbf{Jitter and Timing Sensitivity:} Timing margin is
        quantified by the second derivative (curvature) around the strobe point:
        \begin{equation}
            y(T_s + \Delta t) \approx y(T_s) + \frac{1}{2} y''(T_s) \Delta t^2
            \label{eq:strobe_instant_curvature}
        \end{equation}
        A flatter trajectory bundle around the strobe instant
        guarantees high robustness against transceiver clock jitter.
\end{enumerate}

\end{enumerate}
