\section{Multipath Propagation, Fractional Delay, and Intersymbol
Interference in Over-the-Air Computation}
\label{sec:multipath_isi_chapter}
While \cref{sec:ideal_ota_baseline,eq:mac_sum} established the
foundational premise of \gls{ota} computing under
an idealized, flat-fading \gls{awgn} channel
\cite{sahin_survey_2023,nazer_reliable_nodate}, practical wireless
propagation environments diverge substantially
from this benchmark
\cite{perez-neira_waveforms_2025,zhu_over--air_2024}. Electromagnetic
waves emitted simultaneously by
distributed \gls{ed}s propagate across diverse
physical paths, undergoing reflection, diffraction, and scattering
off terrain, obstacles, and structural boundaries.
This phenomenon introduces multipath dispersion and unresolvable
fractional time delays across arriving wavefronts.
In conventional point-to-point digital communications, such
channel-induced dispersion causes adjacent symbols
to overlap in time, creating \gls{isi}. In \gls{ota} networks, where
computation is executed natively through
analog waveform superposition in the air, the implications of
\gls{isi} are profoundly more severe: dispersion
destroys the precise temporal alignment required for nomographic
multi-access superposition, directly corrupting
the spatial aggregation arithmetic
\cite{goldenbaum_robust_2013,goldenbaum_analyzing_2011}.

This chapter presents a comprehensive theoretical formulation,
algorithmic discrete-time modeling, block-level
Simulink architecture, and experimental diagnostic framework for
analyzing \gls{ota} computation under multipath
propagation and fractional delay. We derive the closed-form
mathematical expression for the resulting irreducible
\gls{mse} error floor, demonstrate how it scales quadratically with
the multipath echo gain ($g^2$), evaluate the
sub-sample fidelity of polynomial Farrow fractional delay
interpolation, and validate the system dynamics across
multi-rate sample-based Simulink architectures, animated eye
patterns, and constellation diagrams.

\subsection{Continuous-Time Formulation and Multipath Channel Model}
\label{subsec:continuous_time_multipath_model}
Consider an Over-the-Air computation network comprising $K$
distributed sensor nodes deployed across an environment
(such as an agricultural sensory grid), concurrently transmitting
localized scalar measurements to a central \gls{fc}.
Each sensor node $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ generates a discrete
sequence of zero-mean, uncorrelated information symbols
$x_k[m]$ with symbol duration $T_s$ and statistical variance:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[x_k[m]] = 0, \quad \mathbb{E}[x_k[m] x_{k'}[m']] =
    \sigma_x^2 \, \delta_{k,k'} \, \delta_{m,m'}
    \label{eq:sensor_symbol_statistics}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{i,j}$ denotes the Kronecker delta function. In uncoded
analog \gls{pam}, each device directly maps
its local sensor reading to the amplitude of a transmitted
continuous-time pulse. Let $p(t)$ denote an identical
\gls{rrc} pulse-shaping filter implemented at each transmitter,
possessing roll-off factor $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ and
unit energy normalization:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p^2(t) \, dt = 1
    \label{eq:rrc_continuous_unit_energy_isi}
\end{equation}
The continuous-time baseband waveform radiated by the $k$-th device
is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    s_k(t) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, p(t - m T_s)
    \label{eq:single_node_transmit_signal}
\end{equation}
By linear electromagnetic superposition over the shared wireless
medium, the aggregate transmitted waveform
arriving over the line-of-sight path is:
\begin{equation}
    s(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K s_k(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K
    \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, p(t - m T_s)
    \label{eq:aggregate_transmit_waveform}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Two-Ray Multipath Channel Profile}
\label{subsubsec:two_ray_channel_profile}
To rigorously isolate the fundamental degradation induced by
multipath dispersion without obscuring the underlying
mechanics behind complex fading distributions, the propagation medium
is modeled as a two-ray multipath channel.
The channel consists of a dominant direct path and an unresolved
secondary specular reflection (echo) characterized
by an attenuation gain $g \in [0, 1]$ and a fractional time delay
$\tau \in \mathbb{R}^+$:
\begin{equation}
    h(t) = \delta(t) + g \, \delta(t - \tau)
    \label{eq:two_ray_impulse_response}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\cdot)$ represents the Dirac delta distribution.
Expressing the delay relative to the symbol interval
yields the normalized fractional delay $\tau_{\text{norm}} = \tau /
T_s$. The continuous-time signal arriving at the
receive antenna of the \gls{fc} prior to noise corruption is governed
by the linear continuous convolution:
\begin{equation}
    r(t) = (s * h)(t) + w(t) = s(t) + g \, s(t - \tau) + w(t)
    \label{eq:continuous_received_waveform}
\end{equation}
where $w(t)$ denotes additive white Gaussian noise with two-sided
power spectral density $N_0 / 2$.

\subsubsection{Receiver Matched Filtering and Symbol Strobing}
\label{subsubsec:matched_filtering_strobing}
At the \gls{fc}, the received waveform $r(t)$ passes through a
matched filter identical to the transmitter pulse
shape, $p^*(-t) = p(t)$. By design, the continuous-time convolution
of two matched \gls{rrc} filters yields the
standard Nyquist \gls{rc} pulse $r_c(t) = (p *
p)(t)$~\cite{gandhi_implementation_2013,perez-neira_waveforms_2025}:
\begin{equation}
    r_c(t) = \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{t}{T_s}\right)
    \frac{\cos\left(\frac{\pi \alpha t}{T_s}\right)}{1 -
    \left(\frac{2\alpha t}{T_s}\right)^2}
    \label{eq:raised_cosine_analytical_formula}
\end{equation}
The output of the matched filter before sampling is given by:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = (r * p)(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m]
    \, r_c(t - m T_s) + g \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}
    x_k[m] \, r_c(t - m T_s - \tau) + \tilde{w}(t)
    \label{eq:matched_filter_output_continuous_isi}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{w}(t) = (w * p)(t)$ represents the filtered Gaussian
noise process.

Sampling $y(t)$ at the nominal symbol strobe instants $t = n T_s$
yields the discrete-time observation sequence:
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n] \, r_c(0) + \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{m \neq
    n} x_k[m] \, r_c((n-m)T_s) + g \sum_{k=1}^K
    \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, r_c((n-m)T_s - \tau) + \tilde{w}[n]
    \label{eq:discrete_sampled_matched_output}
\end{equation}
By the first Nyquist criterion for zero \gls{isi}, the direct-path
\gls{rc} pulse satisfies $r_c(0) = 1$ and
$r_c(l T_s) = 0$ for all non-zero integers $l \neq 0$. Consequently,
the direct-path summation collapses cleanly to
the desired instantaneous sum, whereas the multipath echo introduces
non-vanishing cross-symbol leakage:
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n]}_{\text{Desired
    Superposition}} + \underbrace{g \sum_{k=1}^K
        \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x_k[m] \, r_c((n-m)T_s -
    \tau)}_{\text{Multipath-Induced Intersymbol Interference}} + \tilde{w}[n]
    \label{eq:multipath_sampled_superposition}
\end{equation}

To estimate the true spatial arithmetic mean of the distributed
sensors, $\mu[n] = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K x_k[n]$,
the \gls{fc} applies a standard post-scaling factor of $1/K$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mu}[n] = \frac{y[n]}{K} = \mu[n] + g
    \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \mu[m] \, r_c((n-m)T_s - \tau) +
    \frac{\tilde{w}[n]}{K}
    \label{eq:estimated_spatial_mean}
\end{equation}
\cref{eq:estimated_spatial_mean} explicitly demonstrates the
fundamental failure mode of analog Over-the-Air
computing in dispersive channels: even when the line-of-sight path is
perfectly synchronized, the fractional
multipath echo smears previous and subsequent spatial averages
$\mu[m]$ into the current estimation strobe $n$.

\subsection{Analytical Derivation of the Multipath Error Floor}
\label{subsec:analytical_error_floor_derivation}
To quantify the performance boundary of \gls{ota} computation under
multipath interference, we examine the instantaneous
computation error at the \gls{fc}, defined as $e[n] = \hat{\mu}[n] - \mu[n]$:
\begin{equation}
    e[n] = g \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \mu[m] \, r_c((n-m)T_s - \tau)
    + \frac{\tilde{w}[n]}{K}
    \label{eq:total_computation_error}
\end{equation}
The overall computation \gls{mse} is given by the statistical
expectation of the squared error:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MSE}(g, \tau, \text{SNR}) = \mathbb{E}\left[ e^2[n] \right]
    \label{eq:mse_definition}
\end{equation}
Because the information symbols $x_k[m]$ and the additive channel
noise $\tilde{w}[n]$ are statistically independent
and mutually uncorrelated, the cross-terms vanish under expectation,
yielding a complete decoupling between the
thermal noise variance and the multipath interference term:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MSE}(g, \tau, \text{SNR}) =
    \operatorname{MSE}_{\text{noise}}(\text{SNR}) +
    \operatorname{MSE}_{\text{floor}}(g, \tau)
    \label{eq:mse_decoupling}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Closed-Form Asymptotic Derivation in the High-SNR Regime}
\label{subsubsec:closed_form_floor}
In the high-\gls{snr} regime, thermal noise contributions vanish
($\tilde{w}[n] \to 0$), leaving the residual error
entirely dictated by the multipath echo:
\begin{equation}
    e_{\text{floor}}[n] = g \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \mu[m] \,
    r_c((n-m)T_s - \tau)
    \label{eq:floor_error_definition}
\end{equation}
Recalling from \cref{eq:sensor_symbol_statistics} that the spatial
averages have zero mean and autocovariance:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[\mu[m]] = 0, \quad \mathbb{E}[\mu[m]\mu[m']] =
    \frac{\sigma_x^2}{K} \, \delta_{m,m'}
    \label{eq:spatial_average_covariance}
\end{equation}
the expected squared error floor expands into a double summation:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MSE}_{\text{floor}}(g, \tau) = g^2
    \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{m'=-\infty}^{\infty}
    \mathbb{E}[\mu[m]\mu[m']] \, r_c((n-m)T_s - \tau) \, r_c((n-m')T_s - \tau)
    \label{eq:floor_double_sum}
\end{equation}
Substituting \cref{eq:spatial_average_covariance} collapses the inner
summation across $m' = m$:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MSE}_{\text{floor}}(g, \tau) = \frac{g^2
    \sigma_x^2}{K} \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} r_c^2((n-m)T_s - \tau)
    \label{eq:floor_single_sum_m}
\end{equation}
Applying the change of summation index $l = n - m$ and defining the
normalized fractional delay $\tau_{\text{norm}} = \tau / T_s$:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MSE}_{\text{floor}}(g, \tau) = \frac{g^2
    \sigma_x^2}{K} \sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty} r_c^2\big((l -
    \tau_{\text{norm}}) T_s\big)
    \label{eq:floor_index_l}
\end{equation}
Evaluating the analytical \gls{rc} pulse equation from
\cref{eq:raised_cosine_analytical_formula} at the shifted instants
$t = (l - \tau_{\text{norm}}) T_s$ produces the exact closed-form
expression for the **irreducible multipath error floor**:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\operatorname{MSE}_{\text{floor}}(g, \tau) = \frac{g^2
        \sigma_x^2}{K} \sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty} \left[
            \operatorname{sinc}(l - \tau_{\text{norm}}) \, \frac{\cos\big(\pi
            \alpha (l - \tau_{\text{norm}})\big)}{1 - 4 \alpha^2 (l -
    \tau_{\text{norm}})^2} \right]^2}
    \label{eq:closed_form_error_floor}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Implications and Asymptotic Regimes}
\label{subsubsec:implications_asymptotic_regimes}
Inspection of \cref{eq:closed_form_error_floor,eq:mse_decoupling}
reveals three critical insights regarding Over-the-Air
computation performance in multipath environments:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Quadratic Echo Scaling ($g^2$ Floor):} The error
        floor is strictly proportional to $g^2$. Even weak
        multipath reflections ($g = 0.1$) introduce an asymptotic
        plateau that prevents the \gls{mse} from decreasing toward zero,
        regardless of how much transmit power or \gls{snr} is allocated.
    \item \textbf{Role of Pulse Roll-Off Factor ($\alpha$):} The
        asymptotic tail decay of a Raised Cosine pulse is
        governed by $\mathcal{O}(|t|^{-3})$ for $\alpha > 0$,
        compared to the sluggish $\mathcal{O}(|t|^{-1})$ decay of a pure
        sinc pulse ($\alpha = 0$). Consequently, increasing the
        roll-off factor $\alpha \to 1.0$ accelerates the attenuation of
        adjacent pulse tails, suppressing the magnitude of the
        summation in \cref{eq:closed_form_error_floor} and lowering the
        error floor at the expense of excess spectral bandwidth.
    \item \textbf{Low-SNR vs. High-SNR Convergence:} At low
        \gls{snr}, the noise variance $\sigma_w^2 / K$ dominates,
        forcing all curves across arbitrary values of $g$ to converge
        along the classic $1/\text{SNR}$ slope. As \gls{snr} increases,
        thermal noise recedes, and the curves diverge toward their
        respective horizontal plateaus determined exclusively by $g^2$.
        When $g = 0$, the floor collapses, recovering the ideal
        single-path bound established in \cref{eq:ideal_mse_scaling}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Nomographic Functions Under Multipath Dispersion}
\label{subsubsec:nomographic_functions_isi}
The impact of multipath \gls{isi} extends beyond linear arithmetic
averaging to general nomographic
functions~\cite{goldenbaum_nomographic_2015,goldenbaum_analyzing_2011}.
Consider a
distributed sensor network estimating the geometric mean of positive
physical readings $v_k[m] > 0$:
\begin{equation}
    f(v_1, \dots, v_K) = \left( \prod_{k=1}^K v_k
    \right)^{\frac{1}{K}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \ln(v_k) \right)
    \label{eq:geometric_mean_nomographic}
\end{equation}
To evaluate this function over the air, each \gls{ed} executes a
pre-processing mapping $\phi(v_k) = \ln(v_k)$, while the
\gls{fc} applies a non-linear post-processing function $\psi(z) =
\exp(z / K)$. In the presence of the multipath echo
$g \delta(t - \tau)$, the received argument entering the post-processor becomes:
\begin{equation}
    z[n] = \sum_{k=1}^K \ln(v_k[n]) + g \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{m}
    \ln(v_k[m]) \, r_c((n-m)T_s - \tau) + \tilde{w}[n]
    \label{eq:nomographic_argument_corrupted}
\end{equation}
Because the post-processing exponential map $\psi(\cdot)$ is convex
and non-linear, the additive multipath interference
$e_{\text{isi}}[n]$ does not simply add a bias to the output; rather,
it enters the exponent:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{f}[n] = \exp\left( \frac{z[n]}{K} \right) = f[n] \cdot
    \exp\left( \frac{e_{\text{isi}}[n] + \tilde{w}[n]}{K} \right)
    \label{eq:multiplicative_nomographic_distortion}
\end{equation}
This demonstrates that for non-linear nomographic AirComp targets,
multipath \gls{isi} converts into a multiplicative
distortion factor, underscoring the urgent necessity of rigorous
channel equalization and pulse-shaping optimization.

\subsection{Discrete-Time MATLAB Simulation and Waveform Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:matlab_simulation_and_diagnostics}
To emulate the continuous-time multipath propagation model within a
discrete-time computational framework, fractional
sample shifts cannot be implemented using integer array indexing.
Instead, discrete-time simulators must employ
sub-sample interpolation filters.

\subsubsection{Farrow Fractional Delay Filter Order Analysis}
\label{subsubsec:farrow_filter_analysis}
A critical trap in simulating fractional delays is the use of linear
interpolation ($y[n] = (1-\mu)x[n] + \mu x[n+1]$).
In the frequency domain, linear interpolation exhibits a triangular
impulse response and a $\operatorname{sinc}^2(f)$
magnitude transfer function. This introduces severe passband droop
near the spectral edges of the \gls{rrc} pulse,
producing artificial distortion that confounds channel physics with
interpolation error.

To eliminate this artifact, a Lagrange Farrow fractional delay
structure is employed. The $(L+1)$-tap Farrow filter
approximates an arbitrary sub-sample delay $d = \lfloor L/2 \rfloor +
\mu$ (where $\mu \in [0, 1)$) via polynomial
interpolation kernels:
\begin{equation}
    h_L[n; \mu] = \prod_{\substack{k=0 \\ k \neq n}}^{L} \frac{d -
    k}{n - k}, \quad n \in \{0, 1, \dots, L\}
    \label{eq:farrow_lagrange_kernel}
\end{equation}
The discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of the resulting impulse
response is $H_L(e^{j\omega}; \mu) = \sum_{n=0}^{L} h_L[n; \mu]
e^{-j\omega n}$.
The magnitude droop in decibels and the phase delay error $\Delta
\tau_p$ in samples are defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Droop}_{\text{dB}}(\omega) = 20 \log_{10} \left|
    H_L(e^{j\omega}; \mu) \right|, \quad \Delta \tau_p(\omega) =
    -\frac{\operatorname{unwrap}(\arg H_L(e^{j\omega}; \mu))}{\omega} - d
    \label{eq:farrow_droop_and_phase_error}
\end{equation}

For an \gls{rrc} pulse with $\alpha = 0.5$ sampled at $\text{SPS} =
12$, the transmission band edge occupies
$f_{\text{edge}} = \frac{1+\alpha}{2 \cdot \text{SPS}} = 0.0625$
cycles/sample ($\omega_{\text{edge}} \approx 0.3927$ rad/sample).
\cref{tab:farrow_order_comparison} evaluates the magnitude droop and
phase delay error across polynomial orders $L = 1$ to $5$
at this critical band edge.

\subsubsection{High-Performance MATLAB Simulation Implementation}
\label{subsubsec:matlab_code_implementation}
In addition to filter order selection, two structural pitfalls must
be addressed when simulating multi-sensor Over-the-Air networks:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{State Memory Persistence:} MATLAB system objects
        (such as \texttt{dsp.VariableFractionalDelay}) retain internal
        tap registers across consecutive function calls. Invoking the
        object iteratively across sensors without executing
        \texttt{reset(fracDelay)} leaks the trailing samples of
        sensor $k$ into the onset of sensor $k+1$.
    \item \textbf{Superposition Computational Efficiency:} Because
        convolution, fractional delay, and summation are linear
        time-invariant (LTI) operations, the multi-sensor summation
        can be performed natively prior to applying the channel delay:
        \begin{equation}
            \sum_{k=1}^K \big( s_k(t) + g \, s_k(t - \tau) \big) =
            \left( \sum_{k=1}^K s_k(t) \right) + g \left(
            \sum_{k=1}^K s_k(t - \tau) \right)
            \label{eq:lti_superposition_property}
        \end{equation}
        Exploiting this property reduces the execution complexity
        from $\mathcal{O}(K)$ fractional delay calls to a single
        $\mathcal{O}(1)$ invocation.
\end{itemize}

\cref{lst:matlab_multipath_aircomp} presents the complete,
high-performance MATLAB script modeling the two-ray multipath AirComp channel.

    \begin{lstlisting}[style=Matlab-editor, caption={MATLAB implementation of Over-the-Air computation under two-ray multipath fading with Farrow fractional delay filtering.}, label={lst:matlab_multipath_aircomp}]
% Over-the-Air Computation under Two-Ray Multipath Fading
clc; clear; close all;

%% 1. Simulation Parameters
K          = 20;                      % Number of participating sensor nodes
N          = 1000;                    % Symbol sequence length per sensor
SNR_dB     = -20:2:20;                % Channel SNR sweep (dB)
alpha_val  = 0.50;                    % RRC roll-off factor
sps_val    = 12;                      % Samples per symbol (SPS)
span       = 12;                      % Filter span in symbols
tau_frac   = 0.3125;                  % Fractional delay (tau / Ts)
echo_gain  = 0.50;                    % Multipath echo attenuation g

% Sensor data generation (zero-mean normal random readings)
X = randn(K, N);
ideal_target = mean(X, 1);            % Ground-truth arithmetic mean

%% 2. Pulse Shaping and Fractional Delay Objects
rcFilter = rcosdesign(alpha_val, span, sps_val, 'sqrt');
max_delay_samples = ceil(2.0 * sps_val) + 10;

fracDelay = dsp.VariableFractionalDelay(...
    'InterpolationMethod', 'Farrow', ...
    'FilterLength', 4, ...            % Quartic Lagrange Farrow (L = 4)
    'FarrowSmallDelayAction', 'Use off-centered kernel', ...
    'MaximumDelay', max_delay_samples);

%% 3. Transmit Processing and Channel Superposition
tx_upsampled = upsample(X', sps_val);  % Size: (N*sps) x K
tx_filtered  = conv2(tx_upsampled, rcFilter(:), 'same');

% Exploit LTI superposition: sum in the air first
direct_path = sum(tx_filtered, 2);
delay_samples = tau_frac * sps_val;
reset(fracDelay);
echo_path   = fracDelay(direct_path, delay_samples);

% Channel output: direct line-of-sight plus delayed echo
rx_channel  = direct_path + echo_gain * echo_path;

%% 4. Receiver Matched Filtering and Estimation Sweep
mse_results = zeros(length(SNR_dB), 1);
valid_start = ceil(span / 2) + 1;
valid_end   = N - ceil(span / 2);
target_eval = ideal_target(valid_start:valid_end)';

for snr_idx = 1:length(SNR_dB)
    rx_noisy   = awgn(rx_channel, SNR_dB(snr_idx), 'measured');
    rx_matched = conv(rx_noisy, flipud(rcFilter(:)), 'same');
    rx_sampled = downsample(rx_matched, sps_val);

    % Fusion Center estimate (scaled by K)
    est_mean = rx_sampled(valid_start:valid_end) / K;
    mse_results(snr_idx) = immse(est_mean, target_eval);
end
    \end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Discrete-Time Multi-Rate Sample-Based Execution}
\label{subsubsec:multirate_sample_based_dynamics}
In discrete-time \gls{dsp} modeling, multi-rate systems present
intricate architectural subtleties that must be carefully
governed to prevent spurious simulation artifacts
(\cref{fig:sl_multipath_rrc_architecture}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Filter Group Delay Alignment:} An FIR filter of
        order $N_{\text{taps}} = \text{span} \times \text{SPS}$ introduces
        a deterministic group delay of $D = \frac{\text{span} \times
        \text{SPS}}{2}$ samples. In a cascaded matched filter chain
        (Tx RRC and Rx RRC), the cumulative group delay is:
        \begin{equation}
            D_{\text{total}} = 2 \times \left( \frac{\text{span}
            \times \text{SPS}}{2} \right) = \text{span} \times
            \text{SPS} \text{ samples} \equiv \text{span} \text{ symbols}
            \label{eq:filter_group_delay_cascade}
        \end{equation}
        If the reference branch does not incorporate an identical
        delay block ($z^{-\text{span}}$), the subtractor block compares
        symbol $n$ against symbol $n - \text{span}$. For signals
        centered around a common mean ($\mu \approx 27$), subtracting two
        uncorrelated symbols produces an instantaneous squared error
        of $(27 - 0)^2 \approx 729$, causing the running \gls{mse}
        display to report erroneous values ($\approx 659.1$).
        Inserting an integer delay block matching the filter span resolves
        this mismatch completely.
    \item \textbf{Fine Sampling Phase vs. Coarse Symbol Delay:} The
        total delay equation decomposes into coarse symbol delay
        and fine sampling strobe phase:
        \begin{equation}
            D_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{D_{\text{sym}} \times
            \text{SPS}}_{\text{Coarse Symbol Delay}} +
            \underbrace{\phi_{\text{sample}}}_{\text{Fine Sampling Phase}}
            \label{eq:unified_delay_equation}
        \end{equation}
        Adjusting the \texttt{Sample Offset} parameter inside the
        \texttt{Downsample} block selects where within the eye opening
        the receiver takes its decision snapshot. Sampling off-peak
        traces out a steep parabolic U-shaped error curve, verifying
        that sub-sample clock alignment is essential for Nyquist
        zero-ISI cancellation.
    \item \textbf{Transient Accumulator Warm-Up:} The cumulative
        running mean block executes $\bar{e}^2[n] =
        \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n e^2[k]$.
        Because all filter registers initialize to zero, the running
        mean requires an initial warm-up transient to flush out startup
        zeros, tracing out a charging response before settling at the
        true steady-state plateau.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Transceiver Impairments, Synchronization, and Equalization}
\label{subsec:impairments_and_equalization}
To complete the physical characterization of Over-the-Air computing,
the challenges of phase misalignment, carrier frequency
offsets, and hardware implementation are
addressed~\cite{sahin_survey_2023,evgenidis_waveform_2025,xiao_heterogeneous_2025}.

\subsubsection{Transmitter Pre-Equalization and Phase Misalignment}
\label{subsubsec:preequalization_phase_error}
In physical wireless channels, attenuation and phase rotation are
modeled as complex fading coefficients $h_k = \alpha_k e^{j\theta_k}$.
To achieve constructive in-phase superposition, each sensor node
applies zero-forcing transmitter pre-equalization:
\begin{equation}
    w_k = \frac{1}{\hat{h}_k} = \frac{1}{\hat{\alpha}_k e^{j\hat{\theta}_k}}
    \label{eq:preequalization_weight}
\end{equation}
In practical deployments, sensors possess imperfect channel state
information (\gls{csi}). Assuming perfect amplitude estimation
($\hat{\alpha}_k = \alpha_k$) but a residual phase estimation error
$\Delta\theta_k = \hat{\theta}_k - \theta_k$, the received
signal at the \gls{fc} prior to noise is:
\begin{equation}
    y = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k \, w_k \, h_k = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k \left(
    \frac{1}{\alpha_k} e^{-j(\theta_k + \Delta\theta_k)} \right)
    \left( \alpha_k e^{j\theta_k} \right) = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k
    e^{-j\Delta\theta_k}
    \label{eq:phase_misalignment_superposition}
\end{equation}
Applying Euler's identity and the small-angle Taylor expansion
($\cos(\Delta\theta) \approx 1$, $\sin(\Delta\theta) \approx \Delta\theta$):
\begin{equation}
    y \approx \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K x_k}_{\text{Ideal Computation
    (In-Phase)}} \quad - \quad j \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K x_k
    \Delta\theta_k}_{\text{Phase Error Residual (Quadrature)}}
    \label{eq:phase_error_isolation}
\end{equation}
\cref{eq:phase_error_isolation} yields a vital theoretical conclusion
for Over-the-Air computing architectures: because the
cosine function possesses zero first derivative around zero
($\cos(\Delta\theta) \approx 1 - \frac{1}{2}\Delta\theta^2$), minor
phase synchronization errors do not severely corrupt the real
(In-Phase) computation. Instead, the first-order error is
projected entirely into the imaginary (Quadrature) component. By
detecting strictly along the in-phase axis, the \gls{fc}
inherently suppresses first-order phase jitter.

\subsubsection{Carrier Frequency Offset (CFO) and Constellation Rotation}
\label{subsubsec:cfo_effects}
While phase errors project into the quadrature dimension, local
oscillator frequency mismatch between transmitters and the
\gls{fc} introduces a \gls{cfo} $\Delta f = f_{\text{rx}} -
f_{\text{tx}}$. In complex baseband representation, \gls{cfo} imparts a
continuous, time-varying phase rotation:
\begin{equation}
    r(t) = s(t) \, e^{j 2\pi \Delta f t}
    \label{eq:cfo_continuous_rotation}
\end{equation}
At discrete symbol intervals $n$, the phase advances progressively by
$\theta_n = 2\pi \Delta f n T_s$. Consequently, the
one-dimensional real PAM constellation rotates continuously around
the origin, expanding into a circular disk or ring in the
complex $(I, Q)$ plane. Furthermore, the computation error becomes:
\begin{equation}
    |e[n]|^2 = \left| r[n] e^{j 2\pi \Delta f n T_s} - s[n] \right|^2
    = 2 s^2[n] \Big( 1 - \cos(2\pi \Delta f n T_s) \Big)
    \label{eq:cfo_mse_expansion}
\end{equation}
Averaged over time, $\frac{1}{N}\sum \cos(2\pi \Delta f n T_s) \to
0$, causing the \gls{mse} floor to collapse catastrophically
to $\approx 2 \sigma_s^2$. This wipes out the deep notch of the
symbol timing S-curve, demonstrating that frequency synchronization
loops (such as Costas loops or carrier recovery algorithms) are
mandatory prerequisites for analog \gls{ota}
computing~\cite{sahin_survey_2023,perez-neira_waveforms_2025}.

\subsection{Summary of Chapter Findings}
\label{subsec:summary_chapter_findings}
This chapter demonstrated that multipath propagation and unresolvable
fractional delays constitute fundamental physical bottlenecks
for Over-the-Air computation:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The closed-form analytical derivation proved that multipath
        dispersion imposes an irreducible \gls{mse} error floor
        scaling strictly with the square of the echo attenuation ($g^2$).
    \item Farrow filter analysis established that quartic ($L=4$)
        Lagrange interpolation eliminates synthetic simulation droop,
        guaranteeing high-fidelity discrete modeling.
    \item Multi-rate sample-based Simulink simulations corroborated
        the analytical $g^2$ scaling law and revealed critical
        implementation requirements regarding filter group delay
        compensation and downsampler phase alignment.
    \item Eye and constellation diagnostics provided intuitive visual
        confirmation of trajectory shearing, loss of zero-derivative
        strobe plateaus, and DC gain elevation.
\end{itemize}
These conclusions motivate the development of advanced equalization
strategies, adaptive waveform designs, and robust precoding
frameworks to safeguard analog computation over dispersive wireless channels.
